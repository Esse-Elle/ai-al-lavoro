Chapter eleven, the last one: Leonardo AI, the visual studio in detail. How does it work? You describe an image in words, choose a model and a format, and Leonardo generates a few variants. Then you can retouch them, upscale them or animate them. Three things to know: it's an aggregator, meaning it brings together many different models; every generation costs tokens; and on paid plans, images are private.

The workspace has six tools. Image, where you write the prompt and choose model, format, number of images and references. Flow State, which generates dozens of variants to explore a direction. The editor, for edits in words, crops and expansions. The upscaler, to give the final image more resolution and detail. Video, to animate an image. And Blueprints, ready-made flows like a three hundred and sixty degree video of a product.

There are many image models, and each has its speciality. Lucid Origin is the all-rounder, great for concepts and social visuals. Lucid Realism is for photorealism: settings, still life, portraits. Nano Banana is the one for editing an existing image with words. Seedream 4.0 works in native 4K, ideal for advertising and print. Ideogram 3.0 specialises in readable text inside the image. GPT Image is for precise editing and mockups. Flux 2 Pro is for realism with faithful colours. And Phoenix for vector-style illustrations.

For video, too, there's a wide choice. Veo 3.1 is perfect for advertising, when the product has to stay consistent throughout the shot. Kling 3.0 for cinematic camera moves. Kling Omni for editing existing videos while keeping the subjects. Seedance for camera control and several consistent scenes. MiniMax for video with native audio. LTX-2 for 4K. And Motion 2.0 for quick social teasers starting from a still image. Video almost always starts from an image, which becomes the first frame, while the prompt describes the movement.

How do you choose, in practice? For concepts and moodboards, Lucid Origin, then Flow State for variants. For a photorealistic setting, Lucid Realism or Flux. To fix a detail, Nano Banana, with words, no masks. For a poster with text, Ideogram. For print and advertising visuals, Seedream in 4K. For a social teaser, Motion. For an advertising video with the product, Veo or Kling.

An image prompt has six blocks: the subject, the setting, the light, the framing, the style and the format. For example: a modern living room with a low bouclé fabric sofa, in front of a large window overlooking the lake, oak floor, soft morning light, warm tones, wide-angle shot at eye level, editorial interior photography, minimal, photorealistic, sixteen by nine format. A tip: models perform best in English, and if you think in another language, you can ask Claude to translate the prompt from your description.

Text alone isn't enough for consistency, though. Leonardo accepts reference images: style references, to say "make it like this photo"; content references, to start from a shape and a composition; and character references, to keep the same subject across several images. Then there are touch-ups: edits in words, upscaling for the final resolution, and expanding the format from square to landscape.
