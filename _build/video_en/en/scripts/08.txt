Chapter eight: automating the repetitive. This is the part many people have been waiting for. Every marketing department has jobs that repeat the same way. Invoices from agencies and suppliers to forward to whoever approves them. Material requests from retailers. Weekly reports on social media and campaigns. Press reviews and competitor monitoring. Photos from shoots to rename and file. They all have one thing in common: they follow rules you could explain to a new colleague. And if you can explain them, Claude can carry them out.

Every automation, simple or complex, is made of five moves. Trigger: when does it start? Every hour, every Monday, or when an email arrives. Read: what does it look at? The inbox, a folder, a file, a website. Decide: with which rules? A table written by you, along the lines of "if this happens, then do that". Act: what does it do? Forward, save, rename, write a draft. Log: how do you know? It updates a file and sends you a summary. And you? You approve the exceptions and check the log. You always decide the rules.

Let's take the invoice example. Every hour a scheduled task starts. Claude looks at new emails with a PDF and works out whether it's an invoice. It extracts the supplier, number, amount and reference. Then it looks up the supplier in a "who gets what" table. If it finds it, it forwards the invoice to the right person and saves the PDF in the supplier's folder. If it's unsure, or can't find it, it doesn't guess: it sets it aside and flags it to you. At the end it updates an Excel log, and at the end of the day it sends you a summary.

The secret is to separate the rules from the instructions. The rules live in an Excel file: the social agency goes to Maria, in the social invoices folder; the photo studio goes to Luca, in the shoots folder; the trade fair organiser goes to Anna, in the events folder. When a contact person changes, you update the table and the automation stays untouched. And the table also becomes documentation: anyone can understand how it works.

The task prompt, written in plain English, without a single line of code, goes something like this: "Every hour, check the marketing inbox. For every new email with a PDF that looks like an invoice: read the supplier, number, date and amount; look up the supplier in the who gets what file; if you find it, forward it to the contact person and save the PDF in their folder; if you can't find it or you're unsure, do nothing and add it to the list to check; update the invoice log. At six p.m., send me a summary." Those are the five moves, written the way you'd say them to a colleague.

The same pattern works for many other jobs. Requests from retailers: the trigger is a new email, Claude reads what's being asked, decides whether it needs the catalogue, a photo or the price list, and prepares a draft reply with the right link, keeping a list of requests. Careful: it's a draft, a person sends the email. The weekly social report: every Monday at eight, Claude reads the exports in the folder, works out what went up or down, and creates a Doc with charts and commentary. Press review and competitors: every Friday it reads industry sites and competitors' pages, picks what's relevant and writes a summary with sources. And the photo archive: at the end of a shoot it reads the folder, recognises product, finish and setting, and renames and sorts the files.

There are also tools built specifically for automations, which fire instantly and follow rigid rules. They're great for simple flows that never change. Claude, on the other hand, understands texts, PDFs and emails written differently every time, and decides with judgment. Often it pays to combine them. But the five-move method works everywhere.

Six golden rules. Start with a small, frequent job. Write the rules the way you'd explain them to a new colleague. Begin with permissions on Manual and watch for one or two weeks. Always plan for the "I don't know" case: better a flag than a mistake. Keep a log of everything it does. And at the start, never automatic sending, payments or deletions.

And the pipeline to build your first automation on your own. Choose a small, frequent job. Fill in the five moves and the rules table. Go to Scheduled, "Create with Claude", and paste in your design. Let it run on Manual for two weeks, on real cases. Then fix the rules and grant more autonomy. Start small, observe, expand. Never the other way round.
