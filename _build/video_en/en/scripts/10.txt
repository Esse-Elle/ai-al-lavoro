Chapter ten: an events office built with Claude. Here we put together all the building blocks we've seen so far, on a concrete case. The idea is this: picture the events office as a digital team. Each agent has a role: research, communication, logistics, reporting. Each has its own hours: some work every morning, some every Monday, some once the event is over. They all work in the same folder and leave a trace of what they do. And you are the director: you assign, approve, correct. Claude is the engine, with project, skills, agents and scheduled tasks.

At the centre is the Events Office project, linked to its folder, with instructions, suppliers, templates and events. Around it, four blocks. Skills: event brief, run sheet, invitations, report. Agents: venues and suppliers, communication, logistics, analysis. Connectors: mail, calendar, Drive or SharePoint, Excel. And scheduled tasks: the Monday status, the guest list, reminders, the final report. The outputs arrive as Docs, Slides, Design and Excel files.

The events folder has a simple structure: a Read me with rules, tone and a typical budget; a suppliers and venues file, with contacts and prices, which is the office's real asset; templates for briefs, budgets, run sheets and invitations; an Events folder with a subfolder for each event, with brief, guests, photos and report; and an archive for past events.

The pipeline for an event goes from brief to final report. Claude turns the brief into a concept, goals and budget. Then it looks for venues and suppliers and compares quotes in a table. You choose and confirm. Claude prepares the timeline, invitations, posts and poster. Then it keeps the guest list, sends draft reminders and prepares the run sheet for the day. And at the end it writes the report, thank-you notes and press review. Claude prepares, you decide: everything sent to guests and suppliers goes through your approval.

Scheduled tasks are the heart of an office that runs on its own. Every Monday at nine, the status of all events and the week's deadlines. Every day, reading replies to the invitations and updating the guest list. Fourteen, seven and one day before the event, reminders to suppliers, as drafts. The day after, a draft thank-you to guests and collecting the photos. A week later, the report with attendance, costs and results.

The kick-off prompt to paste into the project goes like this: "You are the assistant of the marketing events office. Read the Read me and the suppliers and venues file. For a new event, create its folder with brief, budget and timeline. Keep the guest list updated from emails and forms. Prepare invitations, communications and reminders only as drafts: nothing gets sent without my OK. Every Monday, send me the status of the events."

Setup takes eight steps: create the folder, fill in the suppliers file and the templates, create the project and link the folder, write the instructions, create the skills, connect mail, calendar and drive with IT, create the scheduled tasks on Manual, and test everything on a small event before scaling up. Half a day's work, plus two weeks of running in.

What's left out? Claude organises, writes, checks and remembers. But for online registration and ticketing, QR code check-in at the entrance, payments and attendee apps, you need dedicated tools. If that platform has a connector, Claude can read its data. The message of this chapter is simple: you don't need another AI, you need a well set-up office.
